Section one

How this book works

The weekly loop

Your schedule is unstable on purpose — a random weekday game will occasionally eat a practice. So the week is built so that the session you might lose is never the session that carries new material.

Sunday → teach. It lands the day after Saturday's game, so you coach what you just watched. Every new concept in this book lives in a Sunday session.

Weeknight → sharpen. It sits between Sunday and the next Saturday, so it rehearses and applies. When a Tuesday game wipes it out, you lose reinforcement, not curriculum. Never move a Sunday session's content into a weeknight slot.

Saturday → play. Coach almost nothing on game day. Two instructions, maximum, for the whole match.

The 90-minute template, every single time

The block structure never changes across all sixteen sessions. Eight-year-olds settle faster when the shape of the hour is predictable, and your assistant coaches can run a station off the card without a briefing. Only the content of each block changes.

10:00 · Arrival Game · 10 minStarts the second the first girl arrives. Ball at her feet before her bag hits the ground. Nobody ever waits for practice to "start," and late arrivals just fold in.
20:10 · Ball Mastery · 12 minEvery girl, her own ball, the move of the week. This is the block that closes the skill gap over eight weeks — it is the one thing a beginner can practise alone in a driveway.
30:22 · Water · 3 minNon-negotiable. See the heat section.
40:25 · Skill Block · 20 minThe teaching block. Two stations run in parallel — you take one, your assistant takes the other, and they swap at the ten-minute mark.
50:45 · Water + Team Challenge · 5 minA short, loud, silly competition that doubles as a rest. Pick any from the game bank.
60:50 · Small-Sided Game · 20 minThe skill under real pressure, with one constraint that forces it to show up. This is where learning actually sticks.
71:10 · Scrimmage & Keeper Club · 15 minThe closest thing to Saturday. Keeper rotation happens here.
81:25 · Huddle · 5 minOne thing we did well, one thing for next time, the question of the week, the cheer. Under three minutes of talking.

The three things you never do

Lines. A girl standing in a queue is learning nothing. If an activity has a line of more than two, redesign it or split it into two stations.

Laps. There is no fitness problem at eight years old that a game does not solve better. Never run without a ball, and never run as punishment.

Lectures. Two coaching points per activity, thirty seconds maximum. Ask more than you tell — "where's the space?" beats "go wide" every time, because she has to look to answer.

Section two

Coaching a roster this wide

This is the hardest thing you're being asked to do, and it has a real answer. The trap is thinking you must choose between boring your experienced players and drowning your new ones. You don't — you change what each girl is being asked to do inside the same activity. Every session card in this book carries two coloured strips on each block for exactly this.

Ramp inHow to make this work for a girl who is brand new — so she succeeds at something today. Ramp upHow to make this genuinely hard for a girl who's played for years — so she's still trying at 7:15.

Note the wording. They're "ramp in" and "ramp up," not "easy version" and "hard version, " because eight-year-olds hear everything. You can say these out loud on the field without labelling a child.

Seven moves that actually work

1Never split by abilitySplit by shoe colour, birthday month, or who had cereal for breakfast. The moment you make an "A group," every girl in the room knows which one she's in — including the ones you thought weren't paying attention. Ability differences get handled by the ramps, inside a mixed group.
2Handicap the game, not the childThis is the single best trick you have. "A goal only counts if a girl who hasn't scored yet touched the ball first." Now your best player is hunting for your newest player, of her own accord, and you never had to ask her to share.
3Give strong players a job, not a bigger role"Your job this game is three good passes to Maya — passes she can actually control." A bored eight-year-old with a leadership task stops being bored. She's now solving a harder problem than scoring.
4Start beginners numbers-up3v1 and 4v2 build confidence fast because success is nearly guaranteed. Put the newest girls on the overloaded side for the first repetitions, then even it out once they've had a few wins.
5Many small goals, not one big oneFour cone-goals in a grid means more scoring, more celebration, and more angles to find. Scoring is the drug that brings eight-year-olds back next season. Manufacture as much of it as you can.
6Never eliminate anyoneNo knockout games, ever. The girl who most needs the repetitions is the first one out and then stands watching for six minutes. Replace every elimination mechanic with a re-entry one — three toe-taps and you're back in.
7Praise the attempt, not the outcome"Great decision to take her on" matters more than "great goal," because the attempt is the thing she controls and can repeat. This is the difference between a girl who tries things and a girl who passes it away the moment it arrives.

The size range

You flagged a wide physical range, and at this age that gap is as big as the skill gap. Two adjustments handle most of it:

Design activities that reward change of direction, not strength. Tight grids, gates, and turning games let a small quick girl win. Long open channels and shoulder-to-shoulder duels let the biggest girl win by being biggest, which teaches nobody anything.

Balance small-sided teams by size as well as skill. Two big girls on the same side in a 4v4 makes a game nobody enjoys, including them.

Section three

What 9v9 changes

Nine-a-side is a big format for eight-year-olds, and it creates three problems this book is deliberately built around.

A new girl can disappear. On a field this size she can genuinely go twenty minutes without a touch, and that is precisely how you lose her for next season. The countermeasure is that practice must be the opposite of game day: tiny grids, 3v3 and 4v4, where nobody can hide and everybody touches the ball constantly. If Saturday is her only experience of soccer, Saturday will decide whether she comes back.

You have about three subs. With twelve rostered and nine on the field, equal playing time gets easier to satisfy but heat and fatigue get much harder. Read the heat section as an operational requirement, not a caution.

You will never scrimmage 9v9 at practice. With a dozen girls at training, real shape gets taught by cone walkthrough and numbers-up phase play — 6v4 attacking, 6v6 with target zones — not by playing the game format. Sessions 11 through 14 do this work.

And bunch-ball is the default failure mode. Every eight-year-old chases the ball; it's the most interesting object on the field. On a 9v9 pitch that produces a writhing knot of eighteen girls and nobody in space. "Make the field big" is therefore not a nice idea in this book — it's the season's spine, introduced in Week 6 and reinforced every week after.

The shape: three at the back, three in the middle, two up top

A 3–3–2 with the keeper makes nine. Use it because it produces three clean horizontal lines a child can actually picture and repeat back to you, and because it keeps a defender home when everyone else abandons their post.

Teach it with cones. Put a disc down at each girl's home spot for the first few sessions. The instruction isn't a lecture about shape, it's four words: "go back to your cone." Remove the cones in Week 7 and see if the shape survives.

The one rule that fixes bunch-ball: only one of us goes to the ball. Everybody else gets big. Say it fifty times this season.

Once they can hold 3–3–2 without cones, 3–2–3 is the more attacking version — but don't reach for it until the shape holds.

Section four

Your league rules, turned into coaching

The build-out line is a gift — use it

When your keeper has the ball or you have a goal kick, the opposition must retreat behind the build-out line. That means your team gets roughly ten free seconds with nobody near them, several times a game. Most 8U teams waste it by booting the ball straight back to the other team.

Teach it as a picture, not a tactic. On a goal kick: two girls sprint to the corners of the box, wide as they can get; one girl goes long. The keeper rolls it to whoever has the most grass around her. She takes one touch forward and looks up.

Say this — "When we have it at our goal, we get BIG. Corners and far away."

No punting means your keeper needs a skill, not a leg

Because she can't punt or drop-kick, distribution is a technique you can actually teach in one session: the bowling roll. Step forward, low, roll it along the ground to a girl's feet. It's accurate, it's easy, and eight-year-olds are good at it immediately — which makes it a confidence builder rather than a chore. Introduced Session 8, drilled Session 11.

Equal playing time + a goalkeeper

These two rules interact badly if you're not deliberate, and getting it wrong costs you a player.

Count keeper minutes as playing time. Otherwise whoever volunteers quietly gets shortchanged, and volunteering stops.

Never assign a nervous girl to goal to be "fair." That's the afternoon she decides soccer isn't for her. Fair means everyone gets the offer, not that everyone gets the assignment.

Run a Keeper Club instead. Every girl tries keeper in practice, in low-stakes games where saves are celebrated and goals conceded are ignored. Keep a running list of who has volunteered for Saturday and rotate through it. By Week 5 you will typically have four or five willing keepers, which is more than you need.

Say this — "Who wants to go in goal?" Never: "Ella, you're in goal."

No heading

Heading is out at this age. Coach the alternative from day one so it becomes a habit rather than a correction: chest it, thigh it, or let it bounce and take it on the ground. If a ball is above shoulder height, the right answer is always to let it land.

Section five

Houston heat

This is an operational requirement, not a caution A Sunday afternoon in Houston in late spring, on a 9v9 field, with three subs, is the highest-risk combination on your calendar. Treat the water breaks in the template as fixed points that never get skipped because an activity is going well.
1Water is built in, not asked forThe template has breaks at 0:22 and 0:45, and the Team Challenge block is a rest disguised as a competition. Add a third break in the scrimmage if it's above about 88°F.
2Huddle in shade, alwaysAny time you're talking, move them to shade. It costs thirty seconds and buys you real recovery.
3Cut the scrimmage before you cut the waterIf you're running behind, the block that gets shortened is the last one. Never the breaks.
4Watch for the quiet oneAn overheating eight-year-old goes quiet and stops competing — she doesn't complain. The girl who suddenly drifts to the back of a game she was enjoying gets sat down with water immediately, no questions.
5Push the Sunday session later if you canA 5:00 start in May is a materially different session from a 2:00 start. If the league gives you the choice, take the later slot every time.
6Ice and wet towels in the bagCheap, and the difference between a girl sitting out ten minutes and a girl going home.
Section six

The eight-week arc

The progression runs individual → individual against an opponent → scoring → with a teammate → team shape → winning it back → play. Ball mastery comes first and passing comes late, which is deliberate: at eight, a girl who can keep the ball is more useful than a girl who can pass it away, and technical confidence is what makes everything after it possible.

Section seven · take these to the field

The sixteen sessions

Session 1 · Week 1Sunday — teach

Everybody's First Touch

Ball comfort and belonging. Today has one job and it is not soccer: every girl leaves having touched a ball hundreds of times, learned four names, and laughed out loud. Get this one right and you have eight weeks. Get it wrong and you're recruiting for the rest of the season.

Kit — a ball each, 20 discs, pinnies in two colours, four cone-goals

0:0010 min

Arrival — Sharks & Minnows (no-out version)

Everyone dribbles in a grid; two coaches are sharks trying to kick balls out. If your ball goes out, three toe-taps and you're straight back in. Nobody ever stands watching. Learn a name out loud every time you take a ball.

Ramp inSharks visibly avoid the newest girls for the first minute. Let them get away with it.Ramp upThese girls protect a partner's ball as well as their own.
0:1012 min

Ball Mastery — Toe taps & sole rolls

Own ball, own space. Toe taps, sole rolls side to side, pull the ball back, stop it dead with the sole. Twenty seconds on, ten off. Count out loud as a team so nobody's counting alone.

Ramp inAny contact with the ball counts. Praise the tenth touch as loudly as the hundredth.Ramp upEyes up the whole time — call out how many fingers you're holding up.
0:223 min

Water — in shade

Names again. Go around: name plus favourite animal. It's three minutes and it's the reason they talk to each other in Week 4.

0:2520 min

Skill Block — The Toy Box

Four small zones marked by cone colour, each with one task: toe taps, sole rolls, inside-outside touches, stop-it-dead. Girls roam freely with their own ball; you call the switch every three minutes. No lines, no waiting, no turns.

Say this — "Ball close enough to touch every step."  ·  "Find me and tell me how many fingers."
Ramp inBigger zone, slower pace, just push-and-run is a win. Stand with her and count her reps out loud.Ramp upWeak foot only, or ten clean reps before she's allowed to switch zones.
0:455 min

Water + Team Challenge — Team Total

Sixty seconds, whole team, count every touch out loud as one number. Write it on your hand. You'll beat it in Week 3 and they will remember.

0:5020 min

Small-Sided — 3v3 to Four Goals

Four cone-goals around a small grid, no keepers, three games running at once. Score in any goal. Constraint: after you score, your next goal has to be a different one — which forces heads up and finds the empty space.

Say this — "If a goal is busy, go find an empty one."
Ramp inMake it 4v3 for the side with more new players, and widen their goals.Ramp upMust beat one defender before scoring — no tap-ins.
1:1015 min

Scrimmage & Keeper Club

6v6 free play, no positions, no instructions. Put a pop-up goal at each end and let it be chaos — today is not the day for shape. Ask who wants two minutes in goal; take every volunteer. Cheer every save absurdly loudly and say nothing at all about goals conceded.

1:255 min

Huddle

Name one thing each girl did well — every girl, by name, today of all days. Question of the week: "Who made you laugh today?" Pick a team cheer together and use the same one for eight weeks.

Session 3 · Week 2Sunday — teach

Stop & Go

Changing direction — the first real skill of the season. A girl who can turn away from trouble stops losing the ball, and a girl who stops losing the ball starts enjoying herself. Move of the week: the pullback.

Kit — a ball each, 24 discs, pinnies, two pop-up goals

0:0010 min

Arrival — Body Part Stop

Dribble freely; when you call a body part, stop the ball with it. Sole, knee, elbow, forehead-that-isn't-heading. Silly on purpose.

0:1012 min

Ball Mastery — The pullback

Sole on top of the ball, drag it back, turn the body, go the other way. Demonstrate once, badly and slowly, then let them try fifty times. Do not correct technique for the first two minutes.

Say this — "Foot on top, drag it back, turn and go."
Ramp inWalking pace, ball already stopped. The drag alone is the win.Ramp upPullback and accelerate five yards. Both feet.
0:223 min

Water — in shade

 

0:2520 min

Skill Block — Red Light Corner → Gate Duel

First ten: grid with four cone corners; on the whistle everyone pullbacks and heads the other way. Second ten: pairs at a single gate, both trying to dribble through, using the pullback to escape.

Say this — "The move is for when someone's in front of you — not for showing off in space." (Though showing off in space is also fine.)
Ramp inShe duels a coach who defends at half speed and lets her win the first three.Ramp upLive defender, and she must complete the pullback before she's allowed through the gate.
0:455 min

Water + Team Challenge — Beat the Coach

Coach dribbles, whole team tries to win it. Coach should lose eventually, dramatically.

0:5020 min

Small-Sided — 3v3 Endzone Dribble

No goals. You score by dribbling under control across the opponent's end line. Why: this rewards exactly the skill you just taught, with none of the noise a goal introduces.

Ramp inHer end zone is the entire line, and she gets a two-yard head start on every restart.Ramp upMust use a change of direction inside the last five yards for it to count.

Scrimmage & Keeper Club

6v6. First mention of positions — just "some of us stay back, some of us go forward." No cones yet.

Session 4 · Week 2Weeknight — sharpen

Run With It

The difference between dribbling (ball close, someone near you) and running with the ball (big touches, nobody near you). On a 9v9 field this distinction is worth more than any other technical idea, because there is so much open grass.

Kit — a ball each, 20 discs, pinnies, two pop-up goals

0:0010 min

Arrival — Number Freeze

Dribble; call a number; girls get into groups of that many with their balls, fast. Mixes the group up without you ever choosing partners.

0:1012 min

Ball Mastery — The pullback

Repeat Session 3. Add: pullback, then one big touch and sprint five yards.

Ramp inJust the drag, at walking pace, then a jog.Ramp upWeak foot, then pullback into a change of speed.
0:223 min

Water — in shade

 

0:2520 min

Skill Block — The Highway

Long channels, 25 yards. One big touch, sprint, catch it, again. Two lanes so nobody queues. Race a coach; let them win about half.

Say this — "Nobody near you? Push it and RUN."  ·  "One big touch, then look up."
Ramp inShorter channel, jog pace. She's racing her own last time, not another girl.Ramp upTimed, and she has to beat her own previous run. Add a turn at the far cone.
0:455 min

Water + Team Challenge — Everyone Scores Relay

Team isn't finished until every girl has scored once. Loud, fast, inclusive by construction.

0:5020 min

Small-Sided — 4v4 Long Field

Deliberately long and narrow, so space keeps appearing behind. Constraint: a goal scored after a run of ten-plus yards with the ball counts double.

Session 5 · Week 3Sunday — teach

Take Her On

1v1 attacking — the most important thing an eight-year-old can learn, and the skill that transfers most directly to Saturday. The message all session is bravery over outcome: we do not care if you lose it, we care that you tried to go past her. Move of the week: the inside cut.

Kit — a ball each, 24 discs, pinnies, four cone-goals

0:0010 min

Arrival — Rescue Tag

Two taggers, everyone dribbles. Tagged girls freeze with the ball on their foot; a teammate frees them by passing through their legs. Nobody sits out.

0:1012 min

Ball Mastery — The inside cut

Push across the body with the inside of the foot, change direction sharply, accelerate away. Pair it with last week's pullback so they have two answers.

Ramp inWalking, no defender, exaggerated and slow.Ramp upCut, then explode three steps. Both feet, alternating.
0:223 min

Water — in shade

 

0:2520 min

Skill Block — The 1v1 Ladder

Four small grids side by side, continuous twenty-second duels, new opponent every duel. Winner moves up a grid, loser moves down — but nobody is ever out, so the girl who loses six in a row is still playing and has found the grid where she can compete.

Say this — "Attack her front foot."  ·  "Slow… then GO."
Ramp inThree-yard head start, defender starts passive. Praise every attempt, loudly, regardless of what happens.Ramp upDefender starts live and goal-side; ten seconds to get past.
0:455 min

Water + Team Challenge — Coach's Gauntlet

Every girl gets one attempt to dribble past a coach. Cheer for all twelve.

0:5020 min

Small-Sided — 3v3, Every Restart Is a 1v1

Normal 3v3 to two goals, but every restart begins as a 1v1 from halfway, everyone else waiting behind their line until the first touch.

Ramp inHer 1v1 starts two yards ahead of the defender.Ramp upNo advantage, and she has to beat her opponent before she's allowed to shoot.

Slow Her Down

Appendix

The game bank

Arrival games — 10 min, no setup, join any time

Sharks & MinnowsEveryone dribbles, coaches kick balls out. No-out version: three toe-taps and you're back in immediately. Never play the elimination version.
Traffic LightsGreen go, yellow slow, red stop the ball dead. Teaches speed control and the sole-stop.
Body Part StopCall a body part, stop the ball with it. Deliberately silly — good for a session that needs energy.
Follow the LeaderPairs, both with balls, one leads and one copies, swap at 45 seconds. Your best vehicle for pairing a veteran with a newcomer.
Number FreezeCall a number, girls group up that many. Mixes the roster without you ever picking teams out loud.
Rescue TagTaggers freeze girls; teammates free them by passing through their legs. Nobody sits, ever.

Team challenges — 5 min, doubles as a water break

Team TotalSixty seconds, count every touch as one team number. Beat it later in the season and tell them the old score first.
Beat the CoachCoach dribbles, the team tries to win it. Coach loses eventually, dramatically.
BullseyePass at a cone from eight yards (fifteen later in the season). Count team hits.
Golden GoalOne shot each, count the team total. Everyone shoots, nobody queues long.
Everyone Scores RelayThe team isn't finished until every girl has scored. Inclusive by construction.
Crossbar ChallengeKnock a cone off the goal line from ten yards.
60-Second PassesTeam-wide completed passes in a minute. A running season-long number.
Keeper GauntletCoach in goal, every girl takes one shot. Coach concedes most, theatrically.

If only six or seven girls show up

It will happen, probably more than once. Don't improvise — run the same card, but: keep the arrival game and ball mastery exactly as written (they're individual anyway), run one station instead of two in the skill block, and drop the small-sided game to 3v3 or 2v2. A small session is a gift, not a problem: fewer girls means more touches each, which is the whole objective.

If fifteen show up, the reverse: three stations, and put your third coach on the newest three girls for the whole skill block.

What to hand your assistant coaches

Print the session card and hand them the physical sheet before warm-up, with one sentence: "you've got the second station, ten minutes, swap when I shout." The ramp in and ramp up strips are written so a soccer parent can read them mid-activity and act on them without asking you.

If you have a third coach, don't give them a station. Give them three names — your three newest girls — and one job: make sure those three touch the ball constantly and get told they did something well before they go home. That's the highest-value assignment on the field.

Built for a 9v9 SBMSA 8U girls' season — eight weeks, sixteen sessions, Sunday teach / weeknight sharpen. Assumes a shared half-field, two pop-up goals, pinnies in two colours, roughly twenty-four discs, and a ball per girl. Adjust grid sizes to whatever space you actually get; every activity in this book works smaller.
